# Perspective writing style

Reference: Nature Physics / Nature Materials Perspectives, for example
<https://www.nature.com/nmat/articles?type=perspective>.

The goal is a readable, argument-led synthesis, not a bibliography with prose.

## 1. Thesis first

Every report has one sentence that states the central argument.

```text
The field is moving from X to Y because Z, but A remains unresolved.
```

The thesis must be visible in the abstract, introduction, and outlook.

## 2. Structure

```text
Title: conceptual, not a list of methods
Abstract: context → gap → thesis → what this Perspective covers → outlook
1. Why this question matters now
2. Foundations and current landscape
3. Mechanisms / evidence, organized by idea
4. Tensions, limits and open questions
5. Outlook: what would change the picture
Box (optional): key concepts, methods or a comparison table
References: selective, not exhaustive
```

## 3. Paragraph rules

- One paragraph = one claim.
- First sentence states the claim.
- Evidence follows, not the reverse.
- End with the implication or transition.
- Do not write "Paper A did X. Paper B did Y."
- Group papers by mechanism, not chronology.
- Define jargon on first use.
- Prefer concrete verbs and short sentences.

## 4. Evidence policy

- Every factual claim maps to a verified source.
- Do not generate identifiers or citations.
- Representative citations, not every paper.
- Distinguish:
  - established consensus
  - contested interpretation
  - speculation / outlook
- Keep the L1/L2/L3 fact layer; writing may not override it.

## 5. Figures and tables

- 3–5 figures maximum.
- Figure 1 should explain the central concept, not decorate.
- Each caption begins with the takeaway, then describes the panel.
- Tables compare mechanisms or evidence; no raw dump.
- OA figures require license and attribution.
- Generated charts require data source and parameters.

## 6. Abstract

150–220 words:

```text
1 sentence context
1 sentence gap
1 sentence thesis
2–3 sentences scope and evidence
1 sentence outlook
```

## 7. Editing gates

A draft fails if any answer is no:

- Is there a thesis?
- Does each section make an argument?
- Are citations representative rather than exhaustive?
- Is every claim traceable?
- Are open questions explicit?
- Is the abstract readable without the body?
- Are figures conceptual and captioned with a takeaway?
- Would a non-specialist scientist understand the first two pages?

## 8. Anti-patterns

- literature inventory
- one paragraph per paper
- timeline dump
- citation carpet
- unexplained acronyms
- "many studies show" without a source
- conclusions that repeat the introduction
- figure captions that only label panels
- generated citations

## 9. Editing pass

```text
evidence layer
→ thematic clusters
→ thesis
→ outline
→ section drafts
→ perspective editor
→ citation checker
→ readability check
→ final render
```

The perspective editor may rewrite prose, but may not add facts or references.
